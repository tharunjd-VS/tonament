Every club organiser has seen it. You run an Open doubles event, and the entries are a mix: pairs who started playing last year, regulars who play every evening, and two pairs who play district tournaments. Draw them all together and half the group matches finish 21-6.

The usual paper answer is clever: **split the Open by level**. Beginners play Beginners in the groups, Advanced play Advanced, and only the level winners come together at the end. Everyone gets close matches, and there's still one Open champion.

## How the split works

Give every entry a level — say Beginner, Beginner+, Intermediate and Advanced. Then choose one of two shapes.

### One pool per level: the levels meet in the semis

Each level plays its own groups. The winner of each level goes into the semi-finals:

| Semi-final | |
|---|---|
| Semi 1 | Beginner winner v Beginner+ winner |
| Semi 2 | Intermediate winner v Advanced winner |

The neighbouring levels meet first, so the final is usually between the two strongest pairs — but a Beginner pair having the day of their lives can still reach it.

### Two pools: lower and upper levels meet in the final

With fewer entries, use two pools: Beginner and Beginner+ in the lower pool, Intermediate and Advanced in the upper pool. Each pool plays its own groups and its own semi-finals, and the two pool winners meet only in the final.

:::paper The two-pool sheet
OPEN — Men's Doubles
LOWER: Beginner + Beginner+ (2 groups)
UPPER: Intermediate + Advanced (2 groups)
Top 2 per group → pool semis
((FINAL: lower winner v upper winner))
:::

### More than one through from each group

Take the top two from each group if you want more knockout matches. Each pool then plays off in its own part of the bracket before meeting the others — so the levels still only meet in the semis (one pool per level) or the final (two pools).

## Deciding levels without arguments

This is the part only you can do, and it matters more than the format.

- **Use what you know.** Club regulars, last year's results, who they play with on weeknights.
- **Ask on the entry form** what level the player thinks they are — then adjust. Players under-rate themselves to win a lower level more often than they over-rate.
- **Be ready to move someone.** If a "Beginner" pair wins their first match 21-3, move them up before their next match.

On paper, moving a pair means rewriting two groups' fixtures. On Tonament, you move the entry to the other group until that group has started; both groups' matches are redone and their level is updated.

## What about uneven levels?

Rarely do four levels have the same number of entries. That's fine:

- A level with **only two or three entries** plays a small group — every pair still gets two or three matches.
- **An empty level** simply drops out: with three pools, the lone pool's winner goes straight to the final, and the other two meet in the one semi.
- A level with **many entries** gets more groups, and its group winners play off before meeting the other levels.

## Swapping the semi pairings

Some organisers prefer Beginner v Advanced in one semi and Beginner+ v Intermediate in the other, to keep the strongest pairs apart until the final. Whichever you choose, decide it before the groups finish and announce it, so nobody feels the draw was changed for them. On Tonament you can swap the pairings until the knockout is made.

## Spot entries on the day

A pair walks in at 9:30 with the entry fee. On a level-split event, they need to go into the right level's group — not just the group with a space. Put them in the group they belong in and give them catch-up matches against the pairs already in it.
